<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## The Most Important Part

-   Projects end, but relationships between people do not have to
-   The people who depended on you will be hit by multiple shocks at once
-   Acknowledge that shutdown is a loss, even when voluntary
    -   Team members who spent years on a project deserve to have that recognized

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Total Pain

-   Dame Cicely Saunders founded the modern hospice movement
    and introduced [%g total-pain "total pain" %] [%b Saunders1963 %]
    -   Suffering is never purely physical:
        physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions interact and amplify
-   Project closure involves more than logistics
    -   Professional identity loss, grief over unfinished work, career anxiety, guilt, anger
-   Treating closure as purely administrative leaves people worse off
    and makes it harder for the community to learn

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Psychological First Aid

-   [%b Hobfoll2007 %] identified five principles for crisis response [%b WHO2026 %]:
    1.  Promote a sense of safety
    2.  Calm the emotional environment
    3.  Build self-efficacy and collective efficacy
    4.  Foster connectedness
    5.  Maintain hope

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Psychological First Aid

-   Practical translation: first conversations after a closure announcement
    should not be about logistics
    -   Confirm people are not in immediate danger
    -   Acknowledge what happened rather than minimizing it
    -   Connect people with one another
    -   Identify what is uncertain versus irreversibly decided

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Grief and Its Timing

-   [%b Rando2000 %]: [%g anticipatory-mourning "anticipatory mourning" %] —
    grief that begins before an expected loss
    -   Involves simultaneous processing of past, present, and future losses
    -   More cognitively demanding than grief after the fact
    -   Naming this dynamic helps people manage it
-   [%b Shepherd2003 Shepherd2016 %]: grief intensity after a failed venture
    is proportional to personal identification with the project
    -   Grief recovery is not linear
    -   A retrospective held a month after closure captures more than one held the week it closes

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Practical Care

-   Downstream users deserve advance notice proportional to their dependence
-   Help community members find each other independently of your infrastructure
-   Teams that maintained social contact were more likely to reform; those that dispersed weren't

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Practical Care

-   Engineers assigned to maintain cancelled projects suffer serious morale damage
-   People often feel liberated rather than bereft — give permission for this
-   Small acts of recognition matter disproportionately during difficult transitions
-   A goodbye ceremony, however small, marks the ending

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Continuing Bonds

-   [%g continuing-bonds "Continuing bonds" %] theory [%b Klass1996 %]:
    healthy mourning does not require severing the relationship with what was lost
    -   People who maintain connection through story-telling, souvenirs,
        or integration of values exhibit normal, healthy grief
-   Frame closure around what continues, not only what ends
    -   Archiving, citation, and attribution become genuine acts of preservation,
        not bureaucratic compliance

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## *Plan the People Side*

1.  Who are the people most affected by closing your project, in order of impact?
1.  What does each group need to hear, and by when?
1.  What would a good wake look like for your project?
    What would you want people to remember?

</section>
