<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## What Clinicians Get Wrong

-   Clinicians systematically fail patients by focusing on what medicine *can* do
    rather than asking what the patient actually *wants* [%b Gawande2014 %]
-   Project closure conversations fail the same way
-   Discussions should:
    -   Establish a shared understanding of the situation
    -   Make each party's values and priorities explicit
    -   Negotiate a plan that meets those goals, not just what's technically easiest
-   Focus on the least bad outcome for each stakeholder and what trade-offs they'll accept

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Honest Enough

-   Framing matters: "hibernation" provokes less hostility than "termination"
    -   *If* it is the truth and credible
-   "Tell people what you know, as soon as you know it" — easy to say, hard to do
    -   Information gaps cause more anxiety than difficult truths
    -   But you often *don't* know what's happening
    -   Constantly-changing pronouncements raise the temperature too
-   Information vacuums do more damage than honest announcements [%b Wruck1990 %]

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Mechanics

-   Announce that *you* are stopping, not that the project is shutting down
    -   Invite known successors to take over (no supply chain attack risk)
-   Give real deadlines: 2–6 months between announcement and shutdown
-   Use all announcement channels, not just the README
-   Update the project home page:
    -   Key achievements, current state, archive locations, alternatives for users
-   Don't bother with: tutorials, exhaustive docs, new tests, bug triage

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## The Human Side

-   Have someone outside the project run structured exit interviews
    -   Focus on what should survive, not what went wrong
-   Hold a wake: a combination retrospective and memorial
    -   What went well, what didn't, and what stories people will remember

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Dignity Therapy

<div class="callout" markdown="1">

[%b Chochinov2005 Chochinov2012 %] advocate [%g dignity-therapy "dignity therapy" %]
as a brief, structured intervention for people near the end of life.
The central technique is a guided life-review interview:
what has mattered most, what they are proud of,
and what they want future generations to know.
Edited into a permanent legacy document that extends influence beyond death.

A similar structured exit interview at the end of a project
is more than a conventional "lessons learned" report —
it is personal, narrative, and explicitly oriented toward what should survive.

</div>

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## How Leaders Leave

-   [%b Sonnenfeld1988 %] identified four departure archetypes:
    -   **Monarchs**: refuse to leave, undermine successors
    -   **Generals**: depart reluctantly, maintain ties, attempt to return
    -   **Ambassadors**: leave willingly, remain available, most satisfied
    -   **Governors**: leave cleanly, disengage entirely
-   Recognizing your pattern in advance shapes the handover conversations

</section>
