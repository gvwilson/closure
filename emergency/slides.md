## Abrupt Closure

-   You get one shot and cannot correct course
-   Under time pressure, experts don't compare options:
    they recognize a situation and act on the first workable course of action [%b Klein1998 %]
-   This is [%g recognition-primed-decision-making "recognition-primed decision making" %]
-   Experience handling emergencies in other domains can be misleading
    -   But also more useful than people realize
-   Ask "has anyone seen something like this before?"

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Cosmology Episodes

<div class="callout" markdown="1">

[%b Weick1993 %]: a [%g cosmology-episode "cosmology episode" %] is
a moment when the world stops making sense and actors freeze.
These can occur during abrupt project closures
when governance structures suddenly disappear.

Fallbacks help: personal email addresses, shared documents outside organizational infrastructure.
Risks: only insiders know they exist, and they may be out of date.

</div>

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Step 1: Pause

-   First: establish exactly how much time you have and what constraints are non-negotiable
    -   Organizations keeping shutdown secret backfires: rumors are worse than bad news
-   Spend the first few minutes writing down every *category* of thing to address
    -   Access credentials, data backups, stakeholder notifications, legal obligations…
    -   The list doesn't need to be complete — it just needs to exist

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Step 1: Pause

-   Second: triage ruthlessly with [%g moscow-method "MoSCoW" %]:
    -   **Must**: legal/financial risk if unmet
    -   **Should**: minimum viable record of the work
    -   **Could**: ways for community to find each other
    -   **Won't**: everything else, including "do it properly"

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Attention Narrowing

<div class="callout" markdown="1">

Under acute stress, [%g attention-narrowing "attention narrows" %] [%b Easterbrook1959 Starcke2012 %].
Teams focus on the most visible problem while forgetting entire categories of action.

The MoSCoW list exists precisely to counteract this:
it recovers the categories that stress makes invisible.

</div>

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Step 2: Speak

-   [%b Coombs2019 %] identified three communication errors that increase reputational damage:
    -   **Silence**: stakeholders fill the vacuum with rumour
    -   **Denial**: asserting the crisis is not serious
    -   **Diminishment**: acknowledging it but minimising its severity
-   Rapid, honest, minimal communication is best
    *even when you do not yet know what you will do*
    -   "Here is what we know, here is what we do not know,
        here is when we expect more information"

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Two Kinds of Coping

<div class="callout" markdown="1">

[%b Lazarus1984 %] distinguishes:
[%g problem-focused-coping "problem-focused coping" %]
(addresses the situation) and
[%g emotion-focused-coping "emotion-focused coping" %]
(addresses the person's internal response).

Abrupt project closure combines both.
The person who immediately starts triaging data is not more competent than
the person who needs an hour to absorb what has happened:
they are applying different coping modes.
Managing an unplanned closure means making space for both responses simultaneously.

</div>

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Convergence as a Resource

-   [%b Dynes1970 %] describes four types of groups that form in emergencies:
    -   Established organizations following routines
    -   Organizations extending routines to cover the new situation
    -   Ad hoc groups that form specifically to respond
    -   Emergent groups with no prior or subsequent existence
-   People converge on a crisis without being told to [%b Fritz1957 %]
    -   Translates into unofficial mirrors, public documentation posts, community email threads
    -   If you can support this without endangering yourself, do so

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## The Minimal Action Sequence

Six actions that must happen, in this order, by the day of shutdown:

1.  Copy all data out of institutional infrastructure
2.  Push every repository to a public host
3.  Export issue trackers, wikis, and discussion archives
4.  Send notification to users and contributors
5.  Transfer credentials to someone outside the organization
6.  Post a public archive notice

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Checklist

-   [%b Gawande2009 %]: failures in surgery were not failures of skill
    but failures to perform steps the surgeon already knew needed to be done
    -   The intervention that worked was a checklist
-   The same principle applies directly to unplanned project closure
-   Use a shutdown checklist that:
    -   Is short enough to complete under time pressure
    -   Covers categories, not just individual tasks
    -   Has been walked through at least once before it is needed

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## Selective Application of the Rules

-   [%b Lipsky1980 %]: frontline workers routinely depart from formal rules
    when applying them mechanically would produce worse outcomes
    -   [%g street-level-bureaucracy "Street-level bureaucracy" %]:
        real policy is made in moment-to-moment decisions, not in headquarters
-   When formal procedures are inapplicable, the people present should act within their discretion
    -   Copying data, setting up a forwarding address, sending a brief notice
-   Members of marginalized groups often have more practice navigating hostile bureaucracies

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## When You're the One Being Hit

-   Most participants in a closure are not the ones making the decision
-   Insist on a written record of all conversations
    -   Follow up phone calls with an email summary, CC your personal account
-   Pause before speaking or posting publicly
    -   Have someone you trust review everything first
    -   Don't use someone from the same organization
-   Don't sign any agreement preventing you from speaking about moral or legal concerns
-   This is privileged advice: someone whose visa or income is threatened may not have a choice

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## *Categories Before Tasks*

1.  Working individually,
    write down every *category* of thing
    that would need to be addressed if your project shut down at the end of this week.
1.  Compare your lists with a partner.
1.  Debrief as a group.

</section>

<section class="slide" markdown="1">

## *Triage Under Pressure*

Use Liam's scenario (end-of-week shutdown of an open source tool):

1.  List everything that would need to be done to close the project properly.
1.  Apply MoSCoW: sort each item into must, should, could, or won't,
    given a five-day deadline and one person working half-time.
1.  Identify the single most important thing Liam can do in the first two hours.

</section>
