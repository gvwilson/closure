Closing Time

Succession, shutdown, and survival for small projects

2026-11-01

Introduction

  • Decisions made under stress are worse than decisions made in advance
  • So make critical decisions now

  • Disaster recovery is out of scope for this talk

Set the stage.

Who Decides

  • Every team has decision rules, but they are rarely explicit
    • Write them, or the loudest person wins
  • Default is a BDFL (or PI
  • Martha’s Rules: a proposal passes unless enough people formally object
  • Rules must state suffrage: who can veto, not just who is consulted
  • Few projects have a GOVERNANCE.md
    • Fewer put shutdown criteria in it
  • A documented model: the Apache attic process
    • Three active respondents make a project viable
    • If they can’t release, shutdown begins

Ask, “Who could stop your project tomorrow without asking you?” Usually a grant officer or a manager they had not counted.

The One-Page Advance Directive

  • Medical advance directive says what care you want when you cannot speak for yourself
  • Write one while the project is healthy
    • Conditions for shutdown: specific, observable signals
    • Who has authority to make the call
    • What assets must be preserved
    • What obligations must be honoured
  • A HANDOVER.md written by AI and read by nobody is a fantasy document
    • Written to reassure a funder
  • Give it to a colleague, ask for one realistic scenario it doesn’t handle
    • “The person with authority is unavailable”
    • “Two people with a veto disagree”

Push for observable signals rather than “when funding runs out”. Bad: “when the project is no longer viable”. Good: “no release in twelve months”, “fewer than three active maintainers”.

Inventory Before You Need It

  • Preservation means “usable by someone who was not part of creating it”
  • Capture “why”: an explanation of the problem outlives the API documentation
    • Particularly in an age of AI
  • Two questions per asset:
    • How would someone else find and use this if we vanished tomorrow?
    • What do we do first under time pressure?
  • Handover is an asset transfer, not an entity transfer
    • Move selected assets and obligations, leave unknown liabilities behind
    • Saying what is and is not included protects both sides

Note that the distinction between asset transfer vs. entity transfer depends on legal jurisdiction.

Asset Playbook

Credentials and access
Hand them to a trusted person outside the project first. Institutional accounts die with the institution.
Data
Record provenance, format, units, and known limitations. Copy it out first.
Code
Archive to Zenodo or Software Heritage with a DOI on the final release, so it stays citable. Under time pressure, data before code.

Under time pressure:

  1. Credentials (so you don’t lose access)
  2. Data (because software can be reproduced)
  3. Make repo public (messy is fine)
  4. Issues, mailing list archives, etc.
  5. Everything else

“Messy is fine” is the line people need: public and ugly beats private and perfect. The detailed mechanics of archiving and mirroring are in the disaster section.

Legal and Administrative

  • Most often skipped, but causes the most trouble later
  • Keep a record by email, not chat or calls
    • Memory is imperfect under stress, and people revise what they promised
  • Check who legally owns project assets: don’t assume
    • Universities, companies, and grant-funded projects differ
    • Find the OSPO or IP office if you can
  • Transfer or release trademarks and domains explicitly
  • Close bank accounts, settle residual funds, write final grant report
  • Put exit clauses into contracts at the start, not when you need them
  1. Legal issues are most often skipped because project participants don’t understand the law.
  2. keep the record by email, and find out who legally owns your outputs before you need to know.

Succession Planning

The best case: the project continues, and you have time

Four Ways Leaders Leave

  • Monarchs refuse to go until forced out, then undermine the successor
  • Generals leave reluctantly, keep close ties, and try to come back
  • Ambassadors leave willingly, stay available, and are the most satisfied afterwards
  • Governors leave cleanly for something new and disengage entirely
  • Which one you are is determined by how central the project is to your identity, not by conscious choice

Most founders are Generals but take offence at the description. Point this out, then say that succession planning can help turn Generals into Ambassadors or Governors.

Before You Go

  • Be sure you mean it
    • Letting go is hard on you
    • Not letting go is harder on your successors
  • Move when other people think it is time
    • You will be the last to notice, so ask and listen
  • Say what, when, and why, as soon as you are sure
    • Rumours are worse than news
  • Do not pick your successor by yourself
    • You are not objective, and the project’s needs have changed since it started
    • Involving others builds the community that has to live with the answer

The successor is not necessarily the smartest or most productive person; it is whoever the project needs for whatever the next challenge is. In particular, administrative skills are usually more important during transition than technical skills.

Hand Over, Then Leave

  • The succession plan has two parts
    • Transition: what are you handing over, and when?
    • Final state: what is the successor actually responsible for?
  • Write the first draft alone, and the final draft with your successor
  • Hand over gradually (days, weeks, or months if you can)
  • Take a week’s holiday and leave them in charge: you will find what you forgot to say
  • Credentials move a step ahead of the responsibility
  • Leave when you leave
    • Staying half-in confuses people who are used to asking you
  • Leave for something, even something small
  • Write down what you learned, if only for yourself

Tell them this is hard - make it OK.

Deliberate Closure

“Succession to zero”: the project stops (for now) and you have time to be graceful

This is where the work shifts from triage to communication, and people consistently under-invest in the second one.

Five Stages of Decline

  • Blindness
  • Inaction
  • Faulty action
  • Crisis
  • Dissolution

Go through this quickly.

Announce It Honestly

  • The moment shutdown becomes undeniable is rarely the moment it became inevitable
  • Don’t announce shutdown: announce that you are stopping and invite successors
    • Don’t accept unknown successors: supply chain risk
  • Two to six months between announcement and shutdown if you can
  • Announce on the project’s channels, not just the README
    • Give reasons, timelines, and what happens to assets
    • Gaps cause more anxiety than hard truths
    • Verbal instructions (e.g., on a call) can’t be referred back to
  • Don’t invent certainty: say what you know, and when you will know more

Emphasize the last point (not inventing certainty) and the need to write things down so that everyone has the same story. Suggest adding timestamps to statements and updates.

Graceful Shutdown

  • Write a one-page “last known state”
    • What the project is
    • What works and what doesn’t
    • What lives where
    • Include “things we’ve learned to live with”
  • Update the home page with achievements, state, archive location, and alternatives
    • I.e., give project participants something citable
  • Don’t bother with user tutorials, exhaustive docs, bug triage, etc.
    • You don’t have time
    • And AI can construct that later if there’s need
  • Have someone from outside run structured exit interviews
  • Hold a wake: retrospective and memorial together

The “do not bother” list gives people permission to be gentle on themselves. The learned-to-live-with line is the normalized-deviations point in one sentence.

Abrupt Closure

The project stops on short notice. You get one chance.

This is the least pleasant option, and the one they have least practice at.

Nobody Compares Options Under Pressure

  • Experts under time pressure don’t weigh alternatives
    • They recognise a familiar situation and take the first workable option that comes to mind
    • Experience from other domains can mislead
    • So ask “Has anyone seen something like this before?”
  • Attention narrows
    • Under acute stress you lose whole categories, not individual facts
    • Teams fixate on the visible problem
    • This is not a character flaw: it’s just how brains respond to pressure
  • So the first move is not action: it’s a list

Write categories in paralle with teammates, then compare. Most lists miss things like credentials and legal obligations.

Convergence and Discretion

  • People converge on a crisis without being told to
    • They show up because the project mattered
    • And help if given viable information
  • Four kinds of groups appear:
    • Established organisations sticking to routine
    • Others extending routines
    • Ad hoc groups running a known playbook
    • Emergent groups inventing structure and task
  • Frontline discretion is where policy actually gets made
    • Waiting for approval you cannot obtain is a decision, and usually the wrong one

If the audience includes civil servants, they will quote this back at you. Add the caveat out loud: within your own risk tolerance, and do not endanger yourself.

Pause: Categories, Then MoSCoW

  • First establish the clock and the non-negotiables
    • How much time do you actually have, in days?
    • What constraints will not move?
    • Don’t keep shutdown secret to protect morale: it backfires
  • Then write every category before touching any task
  • Then triage with MoSCoW
    • Must: obligations carrying legal or financial risk if unmet
    • Should: the minimum viable record of the work
    • Could: a way for the community to find each other
    • Won’t: everything else, including “do it properly”

As soon as you add time estimates to your MoSCoW list, you realize you have too much to do. You will be tempted to adjust the estimates rather than moving items. Don’t.

Speak Before You Know the Plan

  • Three errors that reliably increase the damage:
    • Silence: stakeholders fill the vacuum with rumour
    • Denial: asserting the crisis is not serious
    • Diminishment: admitting it but minimising the severity
  • Rapid, honest, minimal communication is best even before you know what you will do
    • “Here is what we know. Here is what we do not know. Here is when we expect more.”
  • If news leaks first: notify your team within thirty minutes, publish within two hours

Then Act, in This Order

  • A README and a runnable script are a viable minimum handover for research software
    • Not a clean repository: a running one
  • Notify funders and anyone with a binding claim before the project goes dark
  • Leave a working forwarding address
  • Do not let guilt or optimism make you promise maintenance you cannot deliver
  • If you are the one being hit, not the one deciding
    • Make a copy somewhere public
    • Hand credentials to someone outside the organisation if it is safe to do so
    • Have someone you trust read anything before you post it

The individual-contributor part matters: most people in the room are not the ones deciding. Say it twice.

Caring for Your People

Projects end; relationships don’t have to.

If you are behind, protect this section and compress the earlier ones: nobody regrets a talk that spent longer on the human side.

Total Pain, and the First Conversation

  • Suffering is never purely physical
    • Psychological, social, and spiritual pain are real, and they amplify each other
  • Identity grief over unfinished work, career anxiety, guilt, and anger
  • The first conversations after an announcement are not about logistics
    • Name what happened instead of minimising it
    • Confirm no one is in immediate danger
    • Remind people they have handled hard things before
    • Give each person one concrete task so nobody feels helpless
    • Keep the team together
    • Do not speculate about worst cases
  • Protect whoever may have caused it: give them a recovery task, not a spanking

WHO’s Psychological First Aid lists safety, calming, efficacy, connectedness, and hope. Say so, so it doesn’t sound improvised.

Two Responses, and When to Talk

  • Problem-focused coping addresses the situation
  • Emotion-focused coping the response
  • People use the first when they feel agency, the second when they don’t
  • People recover at different speeds: joking by Tuesday, still anxious on Friday are both normal
  • Three conversations:
    • Facts within 48 hours
    • “How are we doing?” a week or two later
    • The fuller retrospective later still, because grief interferes with learning
  • Rotate people out of the acute phase even if they insist they are fine
    • Whoever pulled the all-nighter should not write the post-mortem next morning

If you are short of time, keep the “three conversations” cluster; it is the part that changes behaviour. The exhausted-hero point usually gets a laugh of recognition.

Practical Care and Recovery

  • Teams that kept social contact after shutdown were more likely to work together again
  • Do not leave anyone maintaining a cancelled project longer than necessary
  • Watch for survivors’ guilt
    • If your data survived and the neighbouring group lost everything, let people talk about it
    • Offer practical help and/or sympathy
  • Feeling liberated is allowed: give people permission, yourself included
  • Hold a goodbye ceremony, and celebrate the recovery, not just the disaster

The thank-you message after a recovery costs nothing and pays off the next time something goes wrong.

Conclusion

  • We created this guide because nothing like it existed
  • It is incomplete, and we’d like your help
    • Share what you’ve learned
    • Share what hasn’t worked as well
  • Take time to grieve
    • The end of years of work will hurt, even if it is voluntary
    • Your colleagues may be suffering as well
    • And those who haven’t been hit (yet) may have survivors’ guilt
