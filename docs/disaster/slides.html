Disaster Recovery

What a Disaster Is

  • A disaster is not the end of the project
    • An unplanned disruption the team works through and survives
  • In 2025, the US government attacked its own scientific research groups
    • GitHub briefly dropped below 90% availability
  • These events reminded us how fragile research computing can be

The Survival Arc

  • Three stages in how people behave when disaster strikes
    • Denial: refusing to believe the situation is serious
    • Deliberation: paralyzed uncertainty about what to do
    • Decision: action, which may or may not be correct
  • Denial usually consumes far more time than expected
    • Thinking through the scenario before it happens shortens it
  • The gap between a credible sign and the first action will be long
    • Rehearsal converts a novel emergency into a recognized pattern
    • The most common mistake is skipping early stages as premature

Know Your Risks

  • Make a point-form list of every service and asset the team depends on
  • For each item, answer two questions
    • How long can you be without it? (recovery time objective, RTO)
    • How much data can you afford to lose? (recovery point objective, RPO)
  • Identify single points of failure within your control
    • The credit card that pays for cloud services
    • The person with the only key to the freezer

Make and Test a Plan

  • Write one shared document everyone can find in thirty seconds
    • Store it in at least two places that cannot fail at once
  • State disaster declaration criteria in plain language
    • “The dashboard has been offline for more than a day”
  • Include a short numbered checklist for each scenario
  • Test the plan by handing it to the newest member
    • Ask them to execute it without help
    • Every stuck point is a documentation or automation gap
  • This is the antidote to the fantasy document

Back Up and Protect

  • Follow the 3-2-1 rule
    • Three copies of every critical asset
    • On two different types of media
    • With one copy off-site
  • Back up source code to two different forges with automatic mirroring
  • Back up configuration too
    • DNS records, environment variables, pipeline definitions
  • Test a full restore at least once a year
    • About a third of backups fail to restore completely
  • Backups are not preservation
    • Backups protect a continuing project from operational loss
    • Preservation leaves a closing project usable by strangers

Communicate

  • Choose one channel that does not depend on normal infrastructure
    • Agree on a fallback and write it in the plan
  • Pre-write messages and store them with the plan
    • One for the team, users, collaborators, and afterward
  • Designate one person as the communicator
    • Everyone else focuses on fixing the problem
    • Silence erodes trust faster than bad news
    • Update stakeholders at regular intervals, even with “still working on it”

Stabilize, Then Investigate

  • First goal is to contain damage, not find root cause
    • Contain the leak before mopping up
  • Assign clear roles for the incident
    • One person leads the technical response
    • One handles communication
    • Everyone else does what the leads say
  • Keep an incident log with timestamps
    • 14:32 freezer alarm triggered
    • 14:35 temperature rising
    • 14:40 began sample transfer
  • Escalate early
    • Open a support ticket as soon as you suspect it is someone else’s side
  • Review within 48 hours
    • Ask what in the system allowed the incident, not who made a mistake
  • Count the cost
    • A day of downtime has a concrete price
    • That number tells you whether managed services are worth it
