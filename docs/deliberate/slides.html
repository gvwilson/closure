Deliberate Closure

The Scenario

  • The project is not continuing and you have time to plan
  • Sometimes called “succession to zero”
  • Work is less about triage and more about communication and care

When to Close

  • A five-stage model of organizational decline
    • Blindness: warning signs go unrecognized
    • Inaction: signs are seen but nothing changes
    • Faulty action: the wrong remedies are tried
    • Crisis: resources are depleted and options close
    • Dissolution
  • The sunk cost fallacy keeps projects alive past their useful life
  • Shutdown becomes undeniable long after it became inevitable
    • Warning signs were there earlier
    • The stage determines which interventions are still available
  • Clinicians fail patients by focusing on what medicine can do, not what the patient wants
    • Closure conversations often fail the same way
  • Shutdown conditions should already be in the advance directive
    • If not, first write down who decides and under what conditions
    • Distinguish who is consulted from who has a veto
  • Discussions should establish shared understanding and explicit priorities
    • Negotiate a plan that meets those goals
    • Focus on the least bad outcome for each stakeholder

Honest Enough

  • Framing matters
    • “Hibernation” provokes less hostility than “termination”
    • Only if it is true and credible
  • “Tell people what you know, as soon as you know it” is hard
    • Information gaps cause more anxiety than difficult truths
    • Conflicting instructions cause lasting harm
    • But you often do not know what is happening
    • Constantly-changing announcements raise the temperature too
  • This project is probably not your only relationship with others
    • Indirect costs of distress are larger than direct costs
    • Lost contributors, cancelled collaborations, distraction
    • An information vacuum does more damage than an honest announcement

Beginning the Closure

  • Do not start by announcing shutdown
    • Announce that you are stopping work
    • Invite known successors to take over
    • Do not accept successors you do not already know
  • Give users real deadlines and enough time
    • Two to six months between announcement and shutdown
  • Use all announcement channels, not just the repository
    • The home page reaches core members, not the wider circle
  • Tell people reasons, timelines, and what happens to assets

The Middle

  • Update the project home page to list
    • Key achievements
    • Current state: working, broken, or incomplete
    • Where code, data, and results are archived
    • Next steps or alternatives for users
  • Do not bother with
    • User-facing tutorials
    • Exhaustive dependency documentation
    • Extra tests as if for a new release
    • Bug triage

The End

  • Have someone outside the project run structured exit interviews
    • Find out what should survive, not only what went wrong
  • Hold a wake for the project
    • A group retrospective and memorial
    • What went well, what did not, and what stories people remember
